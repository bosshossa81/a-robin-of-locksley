Bicycle Race

Matthew stares me down from the starting line on his seven-speed Schwinn. I try to balance on his left, hanging on the side of my oversized three-speed budget bike my mother bought for me at Walmart three years ago. “Loser has to lick the ground,” we hear Danny call out from atop the monkey bars down one of the long cart paths of the park that slowly turned into loose chips of wood. With the carelessness of a lost boy, he falls forward, wrapping his legs around the bar. His human pendulum begins to count down:

“3,2,1,GO !”

Matthew takes off with ease, effortlessly gliding down our homemade drag strip. Matthew was different, he was fearless. He would scale the sides of the playground just as easily as he would scale the concrete slopes of the old fifty-foot dam that broke up the creek below among the cliffs of the woods we spent our summers in. But I was different, too.

Last week, I wouldn’t accept losing in my baseball game against the uppity Yankees. So, in the bottom of the ninth, I alone stood on third with an encroaching lead, the catcher staring me down after every pitch, threatening to pick me off. The gall. Mikey was at the plate. He hadn’t had a hit all day, and with two outs, it looked like I was our only hope. The count was two balls, one strike. It was now or never. I watched the laces rotate through time while my lead grew to about halfway home. By the time the catcher threw down to third, I was already two-thirds of the way home. As I slid into home, the dry flakes of dust that passed for dirt on our field covered the Dodgers logo on my chest before exploding out in a cloud of bronze smoke. Laughter emerged from the settling dust with the catcher’s mitt in my face, ball inside.

The reminder burns within now as I pedal with such ferocity that it frightens Matthew. He weaves in shock as I fly past him toward awaiting glory. At the finish line of chalk marked at the entrance of the playground, Danny nearly falls, waving his hands in excitement. With victory finally at hand, I lift the front wheel in a celebratory taunt to rub it in as the brief bump of a lone wood chip leads to black.

I wake to see Matthew and Danny standing awkwardly in front of me across the finish line staring not at me but at something next to me. I look over to see my wrist wrapped around the bent handlebar. When the ambulance arrives, the paramedic moves the second wrist, now below the original, and asks if there is any pain. There isn’t really, but then I remember that I have a baseball game later today. I look at my wrist and I cry.
